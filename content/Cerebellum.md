*"Cerebellum"* - Latin for little brain. 
# Functions
1. Coordination, Error Correction in Movement & Motor learning.
2. Regulates time, range, rate, force & direction of movement. (Mechanical & vectorial analysis)
3. Regulation of muscle tone & deep tendon reflexes.
4. Maintenance of Posture & Equilibrium by Flocculonodular Node <- Vestigial
5. Movement of the eye (Flocculonodular Node)

## Comparator of Servomechanism Function of Cerebellum

![[Comparator of Servomechanism Function of Cerebellum.png]]

- **Copy of Motor Command (Efference Copy):**
    - When the motor cortex sends signals down the corticospinal and corticobulbar tracts, a **collateral copy of this motor instruction** is sent to the cerebellum via the **corticopontocerebellar pathway** (mossy fiber input through the middle cerebellar peduncle).        
- **Sensory Feedback:**
    - During movement, **proprioceptive inputs** from muscles, tendons, and joints ascend via spinocerebellar tracts and cuneocerebellar tracts.
    - The **inferior olive** sends **climbing fiber input** (olivocerebellar tract) that encodes timing and error signals.
- **Comparison (Comparator Role):**
    - The cerebellum compares the **intended movement (motor command copy)** with the **actual performance (sensory feedback)**.
    - This is the essence of its role as a **comparator of motor performance**.
- **Error Detection and Correction:**
    - Any mismatch generates an **error signal**, processed through cerebellar cortical circuits and deep nuclei.
    - Corrective signals are sent to motor areas via **cerebellorubrothalamocortical** and **cerebellovestibular/reticular pathways**.
    - Motor cortex integrates this and updates the next motor command → ensuring smooth, coordinated movement.
- **Cyclic Repitition:**
	- When the new motor plan passes down the UMNs, a new collateral copy reaches the cerebellum & the cycle repeats.
# Internal Circuitry
The Cerebellar Cortex is the largest collection of inhibitory neurons in the CNS.
Neurons in cerebellum:
1. Granule Cells (GR) - Only excitatory cell. Stimulates all other cells.
2. Golgi Cells (GC)
3. Basket Cells (BC)
4. Stellate Cells (SC)
5. Purkinje Cells (PC) - Largest

Afferent fibres to the cerebellum are of 2 types - **Climbing Fibres** & **Mossy Fibres**.
- **Climbing Fibres:**
	- Climbing Fibres arise from Inf. Olive.
	- 1 Climbing Fibre synapses w Nuclear Cell & only 1 PC.
	- These fibres twine around the PC and can make upto 300 synapses. When stimulated, a prolonged complex spike in seen instead of a regular AP.
- **Mossy Fibres:**
	- Input from any other part of body than Inf. Olive is carried by **Mossy Fibres**. These supply GRs.
	- 1 Mossy fibre supplies multiple neurons.

![[Pasted image 20251018190109.png]]

- **Excitatory Input via Mossy Fibers**
    - Incoming signals of intended movement reach the cerebellum through **mossy fibers**.
    - Mossy fibers:
        - Excite **deep cerebellar nuclei** → initiate movement.
        - Also excite **granule cells** → these send axons (parallel fibers) to Purkinje cells.
- **Purkinje Cell Inhibition (Rate, Range & Timing Control)**
    - <span style="color:#e0de71;">Granule cells (via parallel fibers) excite <strong>Purkinje cells</strong>.</span>
    - Purkinje cells are **inhibitory** (GABAergic) and project back to **deep cerebellar nuclei**.
    - This inhibition <span style="color:#e0de71;">prevents overshooting of the movement</span> → ensuring proper **rate, range, and timing**.
- **Error Detection & Motor Learning (Role of Climbing Fibers)**
    - <span style="color:#ff4b4b;">Major movement errors (especially during <strong>new skill learning</strong>) are signaled by the <strong>inferior olivary nucleus</strong>.</span>
    - Climbing fibers from the olive make powerful excitatory synapses on Purkinje cells → generate **complex spikes**.
    - <span style="color:#ff4b4b;">Purkinje cells then strongly inhibit the deep nuclei </span> → suppressing the erroneous movement.
    - With repetition → <span style="color:#ff4b4b;"><strong>long-term depression (LTD)</strong></span> at parallel fiber–Purkinje synapses occurs → cerebellum "learns" to avoid repeating wrong movements → **basis of motor learning**.
- <span style="color:#e0de71;"><strong>Feed-Forward Inhibition in case of execution of correct movement</strong></span> **(Unique to Cerebellum)**
    - Mossy fibers → granule cells → parallel fibers also excite **basket and stellate cells**.
    - Basket & stellate cells inhibit Purkinje cells **before** parallel fibers can excite them.
    - This <span style="color:#40c057;">early inhibition prevents excessive Purkinje firing</span> → avoids undue suppression of nuclear activity → ensures **smooth, uninterrupted movement flow**.
    - This phenomenon is termed **feed-forward inhibition**, unique to the cerebellar cortex.

## Cerebellar Dysfunction
Clinical Features:
- **Incoordination**
    - Inability to combine different muscle groups smoothly.
- **Intention Tremor**
    - Tremor appears **during voluntary movement**, especially near the target.
    - Due to delayed error correction by cerebellum.
    - Absent at rest (unlike Parkinsonian tremor).
- **Rebound (Remount) Phenomenon**
    - When resistance to a contracting muscle is suddenly removed, the limb overshoots instead of stopping.
    - Cause: failure of cerebellum to coordinate agonist–antagonist muscle action.
- **Dysmetria**
    - Inability to judge **distance, range, or force** of movement.
    - Patient may overshoot (**hypermetria**) or undershoot (**hypometria**) the target.
    - Tested by **finger–nose test** or **heel–knee test**.
- **Romberg Sign**
    - Cerebellar lesion → patient **sways in all directions** whether eyes are open or closed.
    - (In contrast, in posterior column lesion: sway **increases on eye closure**.)
- **Decomposition of Movement**
    - Complex actions are broken into a series of jerky, irregular steps instead of a smooth motion.
- **Ataxic Gait**
    - Wide-based, unsteady gait.
    - Patient staggers, veers to side of lesion.
    - Resembles a “drunken” gait.
- **Dysdiadochokinesia**
    - Inability to perform **rapid alternating movements** (e.g., pronation–supination of forearm).
    - Due to impaired timing and sequencing of agonist/antagonist contraction.
- **Nystagmus**
    - Involuntary, oscillatory eye movements, especially on lateral gaze.        
    - Due to involvement of vestibulocerebellum.

- Injury to cerebellar hemisphere produces c/f on same side of body
	Reasons:
	- Sensory input via spinocerebellar tracts arise from proprioceptors, ascend & enter inpsilateral side of cerebellum.
	- Efferents from cerebellum reach opp. cortex. From opp. coretex, corticospinal tracts arise that again cross to opp. side of body. Hence, fibres cross twice -> Ipsilateral supply
- Injury to vermis causes bilateral c/f d/t intertwining of fibres.
- Injury to spinocerebellum causes motor incoordination.
- Injury to vestiulocerebellum predominantly affects eye movements & balance.
- Injury to deep cerebellar nuclei causes permanent neurological damage whereas injury to cerebellar cortex may partially recover w time d/t synaptic plasticity.
## **Paradoxical Hypotonia in Cerebellar Lesions**
##### 1. **Normal Role of Cerebellum in Tone**
- **Gamma Loop Regulation**:
- Muscle tone in humans is mainly maintained by the **gamma motor neuron system**.
- Cerebellum (especially **paleocerebellum → fastigial and interposed nuclei**) sends excitatory signals via **reticulospinal and vestibulospinal tracts** to the **gamma motor neurons**.
- This maintains the sensitivity of **muscle spindles**, keeping baseline tone.

##### 2.**Effect of Lesion**
- Cerebellar lesion → **loss of excitatory drive** to gamma motor neurons.
- Muscle spindle sensitivity ↓ → stretch reflexes are sluggish.
- This manifests as **hypotonia** (reduced resistance to passive stretch).

##### 3. **Clinical Features**
- Floppy, pendular limb movements.
- **Pendular knee jerk**: when elicited, leg swings like a pendulum (no check by tone).
- Limbs feel “flabby” or “rag doll” on passive movement.

##### 3. **Why Specially in Humans**
- Human posture relies heavily on **precise regulation of antigravity muscle tone** (bipedal stance).   
- Loss of cerebellar modulation of tone is therefore **more evident in humans** than in quadrupeds.
